How the projections are made

  1. Each series is taken from the agency that produces it (NOAA GML, NASA GISS, NSIDC, University of Colorado Sea Level Research Group, NOAA NCEI). The seasonal cycle is removed where the agency provides a trend column.
  2. A least-squares polynomial is fitted to the chosen window: quadratic for CO₂, sea level and ocean heat, whose growth is accelerating; linear for methane, temperature and Arctic ice. The fit is extended to the horizon year and the residual standard deviation gives the ±1σ band.
  3. NOAA declared an El Niño Advisory on 8 October 2026 (Niño-3.4 at +2.1 °C, more than an 83 per cent chance of a strong to very strong event through early 2027). In the three strongest past events (1997–98, 2015–16, 2023–24) global temperature ran about +0.2 °C above trend and sea level about +5 mm above trend in the following year. The adjustment adds those amounts to the 2027 value and tapers them to zero by the following year.
  4. Ice-sheet mass is shown as cumulative loss since 2002 at NASA's published GRACE rates (Greenland about 266 Gt/yr, Antarctica about 135 Gt/yr); it has no monthly series here, so no band is drawn.

Limitations: a polynomial has no physics in it. It will not foresee a volcanic eruption, a change in emissions policy or a shift in ocean circulation. The window control exists to show how much the answer depends on that choice, which is itself a lesson in how real climate models are evaluated.
